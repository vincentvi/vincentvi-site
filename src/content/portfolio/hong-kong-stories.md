I designed the cover for *Collected Hong Kong Stories* by David T. K. Wong,
published by Blacksmith Books in Hong Kong.

Wong was born in Hong Kong, educated across China, Singapore and Australia,
took degrees in political science and journalism at Stanford, and then worked
as a journalist, an educator, a government official and a businessman before
becoming known chiefly as a writer of short fiction. He founded the creative
writing fellowship that carries his name at the University of East Anglia. This
book collects eighteen stories written across roughly thirty years — every one
of them already published in a magazine or broadcast on radio somewhere in
Britain, America, Hong Kong or Europe before anyone gathered them into a single
volume. They run from the years before the war to the years before the
Handover, and the cast runs from barmen and labourers to jockeys, bureaucrats,
scholars and tycoons.

## The problem

A short-story collection is the hardest cover in publishing.

A novel has one story to signal, one mood, usually one protagonist. This book
has eighteen, spread across sixty years of a city, with no shared plot and no
recurring character. There is no single true image, because any image specific
enough to be interesting is specific to one story out of eighteen and therefore
a lie about the other seventeen.

The genre's two standard escapes are both refusals to read the book. Reach for a
stock city photograph — skyline, junk sail, neon alley — and you have sold the
place as a postcard while saying nothing at all about the writing. Reach for an
abstract literary texture and you have said nothing about anything. Neither
requires having opened the manuscript.

There was also a shelf to survive. This lands on the same Hong Kong table as
thirty other regional titles, and a good half of them have already taken the
harbour view. On that table, whoever reaches for the skyline has lost before
the reader arrives.

## Don't show the city — show the people

So the cover shows figures instead. Several of them, in silhouette, overlapping:
a man with a pipe, a woman, a profile below, a fourth behind. Different ages,
different sexes, different postures, and deliberately no hierarchy — nobody is
positioned as the protagonist, because in this book the protagonist changes
every fifteen pages.

The subject of the cover is not Hong Kong. It is the number of different people
Hong Kong contains. That is what an anthology actually is, and it is the one
thing that stays true across all eighteen stories.

## Made of the city

Then the part that makes it work: each silhouette is filled with the lit
windows of a residential tower.

Hong Kong's truest image was never the skyline seen from the water. It is the
face of a housing block seen from close — hundreds of windows in a grid,
hundreds of lives stacked into one wall, the view you get from your own kitchen
rather than from a tourist boat. Filling the figures with that grid says what
the book says: these people are made of the city, and the city is only ever the
sum of them.

It also solves the anthology problem in a single move. One image, true of all
eighteen stories, precisely because it is about none of them.

The composite is constructed rather than photographed — separate figures
assembled into one overlapping group, with the window grid drawn inside the
silhouettes rather than lifted from a photograph of a building. That matters
for control: real windows come with a real building's proportions and a real
building's light, and either would have dragged one specific place onto a cover
that needed to stand for a whole city.

## The lockup and the ground

Three words, three treatments. `Collected` light, `HONG KONG` heavy caps,
`Stories` italic. On most covers that would read as indecision; here it does a
job, separating the collection from the place from the form, so a browser gets
all three facts in one glance without reading a subtitle. `HONG KONG` carries
the weight because on that table it is the word doing the selling.

The ground is neither black nor neon. It is a mottled sand — the colour of aged
paper, or a wall that has been rained on for forty years. The right register for
stories written across three decades about a city that no longer exists in the
form they describe. A document, not a photograph.

## Scope of work

- **Book cover design** — front cover, spine, and back cover for print

Eighteen stories that each spent years living apart, in other magazines and on
other people's radio stations, before anyone put them between the same two
covers. The cover's job was to make that look like one book without pretending
the stories had ever been one thing — which is why the figures overlap and share
their edges, and why every one of them keeps its own outline.
