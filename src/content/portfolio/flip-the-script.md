I designed the cover, the dust jacket, and the interior layout for *Flip the
Script: Train Your Brain to Breakthrough Your Biggest Barriers and Release
Your Highest Potential* by Coyte Cooper, Ph.D., across both the hardcover and
paperback editions.

Cooper was an NCAA Division I All-American wrestler, then a professor at UNC,
and then neither — he left the university to work as a speaker and performance
coach. Wrestling is worth knowing about him, because a reversal is a wrestling
move before it is a metaphor, and a man who spent his youth learning to turn a
losing position into a winning one wrote a book called *Flip the Script*. The
book itself is a system: ten habits, each aimed at one recurring barrier, each
performing the same manoeuvre on a different problem.

## The problem

Ten chapters doing the same thing ten times can read two ways. As a system, if
the repetition is legible — the reader learns the move, then watches it applied
to new material and starts anticipating it. Or as a treadmill, if the
repetition is merely present — same shape, same length, no accumulating sense
of progress, and the reader quietly stops around chapter four. Nothing in the
manuscript decides which. Typography does.

The cover had a second problem, and an opposite one. This is a self-published
book by a speaker, which means it sells two ways: face-out from his own table
after a keynote, and spine-out from a shelf months later when someone is
scanning a row for something to reread. Those are different jobs. Face-out, on
a shelf where every competitor is shouting in gradients and all-caps, the
advantage goes to whatever looks calm. Spine-out, calm is invisible.

## The logotype

The mark came first: `FL!P`, with the `I` swapped for an exclamation point.

On its own that is a trick, and a well-worn one. It earns its place here
because of what it makes possible — it turns a title into a mark, and a mark
can be reused. So the interior collects on it. Every chapter head sets the same
lockup with a suffix attached and a barrier after it: *FLIPping Self-Defeating
Chatter*. *FLIPping Not Present*. Ten chapters, ten barriers, one verb doing
the work each time.

That is the whole answer to the repetition problem. The reader meets the same
word ten times with a different object bolted on, which is exactly how the book
teaches its move — not by explaining the manoeuvre once but by running it
against ten different opponents until it becomes reflex. The chapter heads stop
being labels and become a conjugation. A cover is usually a lid. This one is
the first line of the instructions.

## The cover

The split brain — grey linework on the left, spectrum splash on the right — is
a licensed stock illustration, and it is the most over-used image on the entire
mindset shelf. It is on the cover because that shelf has a handshake and a book
that refuses to give it does not get picked up. Legibility first.

What I added is the part that argues with it.

Down the left, grey diamonds, scattered and irregular, tumbling away from the
analytical hemisphere. Down the right, coloured circles — same palette as the
splash, but resolved into discrete dots, each one landing on the end of a ruled
line, evenly spaced, marching off the edge of the cover.

That reverses the reading the stock image comes with. The usual version says
grey is order and colour is chaos. Here the grey side is coming apart — sharp,
angular, falling — and the colour side is the one getting organised, one entry
per rule. For a book whose actual claim is that the positive mind is the
disciplined mind rather than the loose one, that reversal is the correct one.
And the ruled lines make it literal: this is a page, and something is being
written on it. Flip the script. Someone is drafting a new one.

The rest of the face is left alone. White, one dark grey, restraint —
everything the shelf isn't.

Then the spine takes all of the colour: a full-bleed spectrum gradient, edge to
edge, type reversed out in white. That inversion is the answer to the two-jobs
problem. In the hand at a signing table the book is a doctor's book, not a hype
book. On a shelf six months later it is the only band of colour in the row. It
whispers face-out and shouts spine-out, and it never has to do both at once.

## The interior

The interior prints in one ink across 240 pages, so every effect has to be
bought with scale and weight rather than colour. That budget gets spent in one
place.

Each chapter closes on a full-page typographic composition — sometimes a
quotation from elsewhere, sometimes Cooper's own closing line set under his
signature. Each one is drawn individually rather than poured into a template:
a thin ruled frame, the sentence broken across mixed sizes and weights, and the
load-bearing phrase reversed out of a solid black bar so the eye lands there
first. Ten chapters, ten separate compositions.

They sit on the verso, so every one of them faces the next chapter's opening on
the recto. That produces the book's rhythm at the spread level: a chapter ends
loud and full-bleed with a single sentence, and the next begins quietly beside
it. Left, right, ten times.

The reading pages are the opposite of the compositions. Narrow justified serif
measure, wide margins, a running head and folio and nothing else, because a
personal development book is read in fifteen-minute sittings and a reader needs
a page they can actually finish.

## Scope of work

- **Book cover design** — logotype, cover art direction, spine
- **Dust jacket design** — full jacket including flaps
- **Interior layout design** — chapter openers, chapter-ending compositions, reading pages
- **Two editions** — hardcover and paperback

A speaker's book is a handshake he can leave behind. Cooper sells from a stage
and then walks off it, and the book has to keep working in a room he is no
longer standing in — which is the real reason the spine got all the colour, and
the real reason the chapter heads had to teach the move rather than just label
it. He can only say it once. The book has to say it ten times.
