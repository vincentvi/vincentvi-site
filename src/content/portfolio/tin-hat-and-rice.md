I designed the cover for *Tin Hats and Rice: A Diary of Life as a Hong Kong
Prisoner of War, 1941–1945* by Barbara Anslow, published by Blacksmith Books
in Hong Kong.

Anslow was twenty-three and working as a shorthand typist for the Hong Kong
government when Japan attacked in December 1941. Her family had arrived from
Britain three years earlier and had chosen to stay through the evacuations,
on the reasoning that going home meant going back under German bombs. After
the surrender they were interned at Stanley Camp for three and a half years.
She worked in the hospital office, taught shorthand, wrote plays for the
children to perform, and kept a diary the entire time. Then she showed it to
nobody for the better part of seventy years. It reached print in 2018, when
she was ninety-nine — 372 pages, still in her voice, still dated.

## The problem

The prisoner-of-war shelf has a uniform, and it is a war memorial: sepia,
khaki, a watchtower, a helmet resting on something, barbed wire silhouetted
against a low sun. It is a visual language built to be solemn, and it is
almost always wrong for a diary.

Wrong because a diary is not a history. Anslow was not writing about the
Second World War; she was writing about Tuesday. The book records the bombing
of the camp by American aircraft, and it records dentistry, roommates,
funerals, hunger and a dolls' house, at roughly the same pitch, because that
is what three and a half years actually feels like from inside. A monument on
the cover promises the reader a war. The book delivers a life, which is
harder and stranger and much more worth reading.

Wrong, too, for a plainer commercial reason. The book sells off a specific
table in Hong Kong bookshops, alongside two dozen other regional histories
that have all reached for the same restrained photographic cover. Solemnity
is the house style there. A book that joins it disappears into it.

## The photograph

Blacksmith supplied the image, and it came with a fact that decided the whole
cover.

The photograph was taken in December 1941, hours before Hong Kong surrendered.
The roll was not developed until 1945, when Anslow was released after three
years and eight months in Stanley. The exposure sat latent in its canister
through the entire internment and came up only when she did.

So the cover treats it as a latent image. The photograph is bleached almost to
white across the field, and returns to full tone only inside the letterforms
of the title. The type is the developer. You cannot see 1941 except through
the words — which is the literal condition of the book, a period nobody could
look at until someone finally printed it.

The second decision is quieter and matters more. The picture is not a war
photograph. It is young people in the sun, one of them in a hat, unhurried, an
ordinary afternoon. Nothing in it knows what is coming. A cover showing barbed
wire tells a reader what happened; a cover showing the afternoon before tells
them what it cost. The camp is not on this book. The thing the camp took is.

## The type

`TIN HATS & RICE` is four syllables containing an entire civilian war — a
helmet and a ration, the two objects that replaced everything else. The title
is doing enough work that it needed no help, so it got size instead:
condensed caps, stacked in three lines, set larger than anything else on that
bookshop table would risk. The subtitle runs across the top as a thin
letterspaced line, where it can carry the dates and the word *diary* without
competing.

The author's name sets in a script, low on the cover. It is a typeface, not a
facsimile of Anslow's hand, and it is not pretending otherwise — but it drops
the register from *history* to *one person*, which is the correct register for
a book of dated entries written by someone who was still alive to sign copies
of it.

Beneath the name, one hairline of barbed wire. That is the genre's entire
vocabulary, reduced to a rule. Everything above it has already said the rest.

## Scope of work

- **Book cover design** — art direction and typography for the published edition

Barbara Anslow signed copies of this at ninety-nine, and turned a hundred not
long after. Her roll of film is on the front of it. Her handwriting, from the
notebooks in the second photograph here, is what the 372 pages inside were set
from. A cover is usually an argument a designer makes on a book's behalf. This
one only had to get out of the way of a woman who had already made it, in
pencil, while it was happening.
