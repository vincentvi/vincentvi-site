I designed the cover, the back cover, and the interior layout for
*The Takeover: The Ultimate Mind Booster*, a personal development book by
George "GK" Koufalis and Brad A. Modrich.

The two of them had been friends for fifteen years before either thought to
write anything down. GK builds companies — direct sales, property, apparel —
and speaks for a living. Modrich spent twenty-five years on the floor of the
health club industry, and learned to read seriously in a federal cell, off a
prison book cart, at the point in his life when nothing else was reaching him.
The book breaks twenty winning factors into a format a reader can act on, and
it turns on a term GK coined: *success files*. The self-development you have
accumulated is the one asset nobody can repossess. The divorce, the business,
the house, the car — all of it can go. The files stay.

## The problem

Neither author is famous. Nobody picks this book up for a name on the jacket,
and no publisher's colophon vouches for it. What these two have instead is the
thing a room registers in the first second — the way they stand, the shape
they are in, the forward lean. Their own book makes the argument explicitly:
posture and presence are what tell the world you are somebody. When they were
interviewed about the book, the hosts spent the opening minutes describing
their bodies before mentioning a single idea in it.

That is the whole design problem. Their only credential is one that does not
survive being printed. A cover is the single place it can be reproduced, so
the brief was never *make a success book look serious*. It was: make a
paperback walk into a room the way its authors do.

Which rules out everything the shelf was doing in 2019. Black grounds, gold
foil, a clenched fist, a suit against a skyline. Those covers are trying to
look powerful, and the result is a genre of books all standing very still.

## The cover

White ground, one green, nothing else.

The green comes from the argument the authors actually make, which is
body-first: get the body right and the mind follows, because when you feel
good you do everything better. That is a claim about vitality, not conquest,
and green is the colour that carries it. The title supplies all the aggression
this book needs — *take over* is not a phrase that requires help — so the
colour is free to do a different job. Two signals, neither repeating the
other.

`TAKE` and `OVER` stack in a heavy condensed grotesque, set large enough to
own the top two-thirds. Beneath them the subtitle sets in a serif small cap,
which slows the eye for one line before the deck arrives.

Then the unusual decision: I put the deck on the front cover, not the back.
Three letterspaced lines, carrying the promise of the success files into the
first glance. Most books hold that copy back. This one cannot afford to,
because of how it actually sells — hand to hand, off a gym counter, across a
table after a keynote, and into a phone camera held at arm's length. A cover
that only works at shelf distance is no use to a book that is mostly seen at
phone distance, and green at eight hundred pixels is still green. Black at
eight hundred pixels is a rectangle.

## The back cover

The back cover is the half of the job most books waste, and this one had to
close the sale on its own.

It opens on a question set in italic, then runs a block of body copy with the
load-bearing phrases lifted out in green — a highlighter someone else has
already run across the page for you. A reader skimming for four seconds gets
the argument; a reader who stops gets the whole thing. Below that, a ruled
heading and a short list of what the book contains, green bullets, one line
each.

Then the two author photographs, side by side, each with its bio.

Those are not vanity and not decoration. The book's foundational claim is that
fitness is the base everything else is built on — mind, work, confidence,
recovery. Showing the authors *is* the evidence for it. On a book whose
credential is physical presence, the portraits are load-bearing structure.

## The interior

The interior prints in one ink, which is the arithmetic of self-publishing:
colour inside would have moved the unit cost past what a book sold hand to
hand can carry. So the identity had to survive into a volume with no green in
it at all.

The bridge is the type. Chapter titles set in the same condensed grotesque as
`TAKE OVER` on the cover — same voice, lights off. A drop cap opens each
chapter, and a folio line carries the book's name across every spread, so the
title stays in the reader's hand on any page they happen to be on.

Twenty factors also changes what the interior is for. The book's own test is
the five grasshoppers: five of them sitting on a branch, one decides to jump,
and you still have five, because deciding is not doing. A reader who has
decided but not yet jumped does not re-read a book front to back. They come
back six months later hunting for the one chapter that unsticks them. So the
interior is built to be entered in the middle — clear chapter openers,
generous space at the top of each, a justified serif measure set for endurance
in the stretches where they do read straight through.

## Scope of work

- **Book cover design** — front cover, spine, and back cover for print
- **Back cover copy layout** — headline, highlighted body copy, contents list, author bios
- **Interior layout design** — chapter openers, reading pages, running folios
- **Print setup** — spine calculation and barcode placement

The green wraps the spine and the bottom edge, so three copies stacked on a
counter read as one green block rather than three white ones — a team stripe
on a sleeve. That matters more than it sounds. A fourteen-dollar paperback
that gets handed across a desk is a business card its authors cannot afford to
have looked past, and being recognisable in a pile is a different problem from
being recognisable on a shelf. This one had to solve both.
